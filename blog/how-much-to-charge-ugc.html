How Much to Charge for UGC in 2026: The Complete Pricing Guide

Last updated: 2026-11 · By UGC Toolkit

A brand asked for your rate, and you would rather not guess. Here is the short version: in 2026, UGC creators charge anywhere from $75 to $1,000+ per video, and most working creators land in the $150–300 range for a standard edited video. Where you fall inside that spread comes down to four variables — your experience, what you deliver, what the brand is allowed to do with it, and whether you also distribute it.

This guide covers all four, with a benchmark table for each. Every figure is based on aggregated public benchmarks — the same data behind the free UGC rate calculator at UGC Toolkit. Read it before your next brand call, or jump straight to the table you need.

What UGC Creators Charge in 2026: Benchmarks by Experience

How much should you charge per UGC video? Start with the baseline: beginners charge $75–250 per video, intermediate creators charge $150–350, advanced creators charge $250–600, and premium creators with brand case studies command $500–1,000+.

Experience tier Who it describes Base rate per video
Beginner Under 6 months, a few paid deals $75–250
Intermediate Solid portfolio, repeat clients $150–350
Advanced Proven results, niche authority $250–600
Premium Niche authority, case studies with brands $500–1,000+

These are base rates — the price of the video itself before any add-on. Everything else in this guide stacks on top of them.

Place yourself honestly. Under six months of creating and a few paid deals makes you a beginner; $75–250 is the defensible range. Intermediate means a solid portfolio and repeat clients — brands come back to you — and $150–350 is normal there. Advanced means proven results in a niche: videos that performed, clients who rebooked, a specialty brands struggle to source on their own. Premium is the rare tier where case studies with recognized brands do the selling; those creators command $500–1,000+ per video.

Two notes on reading the table. First, the ranges overlap on purpose. An experienced beginner and a new intermediate creator can both defensibly quote the same number — the overlap is negotiation room, not a contradiction. Second, most working creators land in the $150–300 range for a standard edited video. If you quote above $300, you are quoting above the typical working rate, and the deal needs a reason: rights, distribution, or a portfolio that closes the gap.

Use the table as a floor, not a verdict. It prices the standard scenario: one video, one revision round, normal turnaround. Rush deadlines, shooting in multiple languages, or buying the product out of pocket are all add-ons on top of your tier — the table tells you where the negotiation starts, not where it ends.

How Content Type Changes Your Rate

How much does the deliverable itself change the price? Raw footage multiplies your base rate ×1.0–1.3, an edited and captioned video ×1.0–1.2, and a video sold with usage rights ×1.25–2.0.

Deliverable Multiplier on base rate What the brand gets
Raw footage ×1.0–1.3 You film, the brand edits
Edited, captioned, ready to post ×1.0–1.2 The standard UGC deliverable
With usage rights ×1.25–2.0 The right to reuse your video in the brand's own ads

The entry that surprises newer creators is raw footage. The instinct says it should cost less — no editing, no captions, less of your time. Brands read it the opposite way: raw, unfiltered clips are the material they want to shape in-house, and they pay a premium of roughly 20–30% over the baseline for access. If a brand asks for your unedited files, that is a line item, not a favor.

Edited video is the default quote. Edited, captioned, and ready to post is what most deals mean by a UGC video, which is why its multiplier sits close to the baseline: the editing is expected, not an extra. Quote this when a brief says nothing about rights or posting.

Usage rights carry the biggest multiplier in the table — ×1.25–2.0 — and they deserve their own section, because usage rights is not one product. It is a ladder of licenses, and the distance between the bottom and the top of that ladder can double your rate on the same video.

Usage Rights: The Biggest Line-Item Upsell

How much extra should usage rights cost? Charge +30–50% for a 30-day license, +50–100% for 90 days to 12 months, and +100% or more for a full buyout — with exclusivity priced separately at another +25–100%.

License Uplift on base rate
30-day usage +30–50%
90 days to 12 months +50–100%
Full buyout +100%+
Exclusivity (add-on) +25–100% on top

The principle is simple: by default, a brand pays you to make a video, not to own it. When a brand wants to run your content in its own ads, reshare it on its channels, or keep using it indefinitely, it is buying a license — and licenses are priced by scope and duration.

This is where UGC separates from commodity content creation. The same intermediate creator quoting $150–350 for an edited video can defensibly quote roughly twice that when a full buyout is on the table. Add exclusivity — agreeing not to work with competing brands for a period — and the number climbs again.

Build one habit now: never bundle usage into your headline rate. Quote the video, then quote the rights as a separate line. Line items are easier to negotiate, and they teach brands that reuse is a product, not a default.

Whitelisting: The Monthly-Fee Model

What should whitelisting cost? Whitelisting adds $100–500/mo per platform, billed monthly on top of your video rate — the same video earning recurring revenue.

Whitelisting is a specific arrangement: the brand runs your video as a paid ad from your own handle. The ad looks native to your account, and native creative tends to outperform obviously sponsored creative. Brands want it for performance. You should want it because it is a subscription, not a one-time fee.

That is why whitelisting is priced per month and per platform — $100–500/mo per platform — rather than as a multiplier on the video. The video rate pays for making the content. The monthly fee pays for the brand to keep borrowing your identity to distribute it. TikTok and Instagram are separate authorizations, billed separately.

In practice: quote the video, then add the monthly fee. A one-time video deal becomes a video plus a recurring charge for as long as the ads run. When the brand stops paying, the authorization stops — that is the arrangement working as designed, not a lost client.

One clarification worth memorizing: whitelisting is not usage rights. Usage rights license the content itself for a fixed term; whitelisting borrows your handle to run paid ads, month after month. One is priced as a percentage uplift, the other as a recurring monthly fee — keep them on separate lines of the same quote.

Influencer Posting: The One Time Followers Matter

Does posting from your own account change your rate? Yes — posting adds a distribution fee on top of your UGC rate: $10–100 at nano size (1K–10K followers), $100–800 at micro (10K–100K), $500–2,000 at mid-tier (100K–500K), and $2,000–8,000 at macro (500K+).

Follower tier Add-on per post
Nano — 1K–10K $10–100
Micro — 10K–100K $100–800
Mid-tier — 100K–500K $500–2,000
Macro — 500K+ $2,000–8,000

The default UGC deal does not include posting. Brands buy the content and run it from their own accounts — that is what separates UGC from influencer marketing, and it is why your follower count usually does not appear in your rate. A brand buying UGC is buying the video, not your reach.

The exception is when you also post from your own account. Now the brand is buying distribution on top of content, and distribution is priced by audience size — anywhere from $10–100 at nano scale to $2,000–8,000 at macro scale. This is the one input where follower count genuinely changes the number.

If a deal includes performance terms, the standard bonus ladder runs $100 at 100K views, $250 at 250K, and $500 at 500K. Treat those as reference points for negotiating bonuses, not as a default to offer unprompted.

Platform choice moves the price the least. Delivery specs differ between TikTok, IG Reels, and YouTube Shorts, and specs can shift the price slightly — but it is the smallest factor in this guide. Do not let it distract from the four that matter.

Three Pricing Mistakes That Cost Creators Money

What are the most expensive UGC pricing mistakes? Quoting one flat number, giving usage rights away by default, and never raising your rate — the three habits that keep creators stuck at beginner pricing.

One flat number. A single quote forces you to renegotiate from scratch whenever scope changes. Line items — video, rights, whitelisting, distribution — give the brand a way to cut scope instead of cutting your rate.

Rights bundled in by default. If usage is included in your base price, the brand has no reason to tell you it wanted a buyout. Quote rights separately every time. The worst case is the brand declines, and you learn what they actually wanted.

A rate that never moves. Your portfolio compounds; a rate frozen at your tenth deal does not. Re-quote repeat clients every 90 days — your rate should grow with your portfolio, and the 90-day mark is when that growth becomes defensible.

How to Price a UGC Deal in Five Steps

How do you turn all of this into a number for tomorrow's email? Work the same five steps every time: set your base tier, apply the content-type multiplier, price the rights, add distribution, then put it in writing — and re-quote on a schedule.

Step 1: Set your base from the experience table. Beginner $75–250, intermediate $150–350, advanced $250–600, premium $500–1,000+. Pick the tier your portfolio can defend in one sentence, not the one you aspire to.

Step 2: Apply the content-type multiplier. Raw footage ×1.0–1.3, edited ×1.0–1.2, with usage rights ×1.25–2.0. Know which deliverable the brand is actually buying before you name a number — "we'll take the raw files too" is a different product than "send it ready to post."

Step 3: Price the rights as a separate line. +30–50% for 30 days, +50–100% for 90 days to a year, +100%+ for a buyout, and +25–100% more for exclusivity.

Step 4: Add distribution. Whitelisting runs $100–500/mo per platform. Posting from your own account adds $10–8,000 depending on follower tier. Both stack on top of the video rate — neither replaces it.

Step 5: Put it in writing, and re-quote every 90 days. A rate that exists only on a call is a rate that erodes. Send the number in writing with every line item visible, and revisit it quarterly — repeat clients expect it, and the creators who ask are the creators who get paid more.
