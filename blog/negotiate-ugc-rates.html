How to Negotiate UGC Rates: Scripts for Asking for More

Last updated: 2026-11

Most UGC creators don't lose money while filming — they lose it in the reply to a brand's email. One sentence ("Okay, I can come down a little") can quietly cut 20–30% off a deal, and every future negotiation with that client inherits the discount. Here is how to negotiate UGC rates without winging it: a floor number to stand on, four word-for-word scripts, answers to the lines brands run on their side, and a follow-up cadence that closes deals instead of chasing them.

Anchor your floor before the negotiation starts

Before you reply to any offer, set your floor from your experience tier — not from the brand's budget, and not from what feels polite. That one number is what keeps every script in this guide calm instead of defensive.

Your floor comes from market data. Based on aggregated public benchmarks, UGC creators charge the following per edited video:

Experience tier Typical base rate per video
Beginner $75–250
Intermediate $150–350
Advanced $250–600
Premium $500–1,000

Three rules for using that table:

  • Your floor is the low end of your tier. A beginner quoting $75–250 per video should not accept $50 for the same scope — the benchmark doesn't support it.
  • Move up a tier when your results justify it. The day you have repeat clients and a solid portfolio, your floor moves from $75 to $150 without filming a single extra video.
  • Quote the full package, not the bare video. Content type and usage rights change the number as much as experience does — run your exact scope through the free UGC rate calculator before you send any number.

Notice the asymmetry working in your favor: your number was built from 300+ community data points. Their counteroffer was built from a budget memo. When you negotiate UGC rates from a sourced floor, you're not being difficult — you're being the better-prepared side of the table.

Four scripts that negotiate for you

These four scripts cover the conversations that decide most of your income: asking for a higher per-video rate, trading usage rights instead of accepting a discount, quoting whitelisting as a monthly fee, and declining a low offer without burning the bridge. Copy them, swap the [Placeholders], and send.

Script 1 — Asking for a higher per-video rate

When: your first quote for a brief, or a re-quote to an existing client (review your rates roughly every 90 days — your rate should grow with your portfolio).

Thanks for the brief — it's a strong fit for my [Niche] work.

My rate for this scope is $[Your Rate] per video, which covers
[Number] edited, ready-to-post videos with 30-day organic usage on
your channels. That number sits in the standard range for [Niche]
creators at my experience level, based on aggregated public
benchmarks — happy to walk you through the breakdown.

If it lands above budget, tell me the ceiling and I'll adjust the
scope so the per-video rate holds.

Why it works: the number arrives with a source attached, which makes it a fact rather than a feeling — and the last line offers to flex the scope, never the price.

Script 2 — Trade usage rights up, never your base rate down

When: a brand likes your quote but asks you to come down. Don't discount — sell a smaller license. Usage rights are a separate line item: 30-day paid usage runs +30–50%, 90 days to 12 months runs +50–100%, and a full buyout is +100% or more (exclusivity is priced separately, +25–100% on top).

Thanks for being straight about the budget — that genuinely helps.

My rate covers production and 30-day organic use on your channels.
If $[Lower Number] is the ceiling, let's right-size the license
instead of the price: drop the 12-month license to a 30-day paid
license at +30%, or keep [Number] videos instead of [Number + 1].
Both versions stay priced at my per-video rate.

Which trade fits your numbers better?

The arithmetic, so you can say it with a straight face: a $200 base with 90-day usage at the low end of the band is $200 + 50% = $300 total. Dropping to a 30-day license at +30% makes it $260 — cheaper for the brand, and your per-video price never moved. You hand them a menu with prices on it, and both options still pay you at your rate.

Script 3 — Quoting whitelisting as a monthly fee

When: the brand wants to run your video as paid ads from your handle (Spark Ads–style whitelisting). This is not a per-video line item — it's a monthly license, typically $100–500/mo per platform.

Happy to set up whitelisting — it's usually when this content
performs best.

One thing to align on: whitelisting is licensed monthly and billed
separately from the video itself. It runs $100–500/mo per platform,
based on aggregated public benchmarks. If you're running both TikTok
and Instagram, that's two monthly licenses.

If it helps, start with the platform where your ad spend is
concentrated, and add the second after the first month's numbers.

Why it works: whitelisting is the same video, recurring revenue — the filming is done, and the license renews. Per-platform framing (two platforms = two fees) is the standard structure, and offering a staggered start makes the bigger number easier to say yes to.

Script 4 — Politely declining a low offer

When: the offer is below your floor and the scope won't stretch. A polite no protects your rate card and leaves the door open.

I appreciate you reaching out — [Product] is genuinely something
I'd enjoy making content for.

At $[Offer], I'd be working below the standard range for this
deliverable, so I'll pass this round. If a budget opens up for
[Scope] at $[Floor Rate]+ per video, I'd love to be considered —
my portfolio is here: [Portfolio Link].

Either way, thanks for making [One Thing You Like About The Brand].

Why it works: the decline is final but warm, the number that would work is stated plainly, and the true compliment at the end signals zero desperation — which is exactly what brands screen for when they come back later. Some will. A brand that returns after a polite no usually returns at your rate.

When brands push back: three lines and the answers

Most pushback is a script on their side too. Answer the frame, not the feeling — and never trade your base rate for the comfort of agreement.

The line What it usually means Your move
"Our budget is fixed." The number, not the scope, is locked. Move the rights tier down, not the base rate: 12-month → 90-day → 30-day usage. A $200 base with 30-day usage at +30% is $260 — a real discount for them, an intact rate card for you.
"Can you include the usage rights for free?" They're testing whether the license is a real line item. It is: production pays for the video, the license pays for distribution. Offer the smallest rung — 30-day paid usage at +30% — never a free bundle.
"Another creator quoted half your rate." Possibly true, possibly a tactic; either way it's a different scope. "Their quote likely covers a different scope — mine covers [Number] edited videos plus 30-day usage, priced from community benchmarks. If you share the deliverables list, I'll tell you where the gap is."

The pattern in all three: you never argue about whether you're worth it. You redirect to scope, license length, and data — the parts of the deal you can price line by line.

The follow-up cadence that closes deals

Follow up every 3–5 business days, cap it at two follow-ups, then move on. Silence after a quote usually means a buried inbox, not a no — brands see hundreds of creator emails a week, and the follow-up is how yours resurfaces.

Four rules that keep you on the right side of persistent:

  • Stay in the original thread. Your quote should sit one scroll away from your follow-up, not behind a new subject line.
  • Every follow-up adds something new — a finished spec piece, an idea for their next campaign, a number. "Just checking in" adds nothing and reads as pressure.
  • Two follow-ups maximum, one channel at a time. If you emailed, don't also DM the same day.
  • Close politely, then log the brand. A closed loop isn't a dead lead: re-approach next quarter with new work and a new hook. Warm re-approaches convert better than fresh cold outreach, at a fraction of the effort.

And when a client signs: put the re-quote date in your calendar. Repeat clients who haven't heard from you in 90 days should hear your new rate before they hear your availability.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I negotiate UGC rates without losing the deal?

Negotiate the scope, not the price. Keep your per-video rate at your experience tier's benchmark ($75–250 for beginners, up to $500–1,000 for premium creators), and flex the surrounding terms instead: fewer videos, a shorter usage window, or a later timeline. Brands that walk away over a market-aligned rate were paying below the market.

What do I say when a brand says the budget is fixed?

Accept the number and move the rights tier instead of your base rate: drop a 12-month license to a 90-day or 30-day window. A $200 base with 30-day usage at +30% totals $260 versus $300 at 90 days — the brand saves real money and your rate card stays intact.

Should I negotiate my base rate or my usage rights?

Your usage rights, almost always. Usage rights are a separate line item — 30-day paid usage adds +30–50%, 90 days to 12 months adds +50–100%, and a full buyout adds +100% or more — while your base rate should only move when your experience tier moves. Trading license length keeps the deal alive without resetting what you're worth per video.

How often should I follow up after sending a quote?

Follow up every 3–5 business days, with a maximum of two follow-ups per deal. Each one should add something new — a finished piece, an idea, a number — and stay in the original email thread. After two replies-worth of silence, close politely and re-approach the next quarter with new work.
